\section{Introduction}
\label{sect:intro}

Binary-star orbits are among the principal routes to dynamical stellar masses. Combining independent relative astrometry and spectroscopy is especially powerful because the sky-plane orbit constrains geometry while a double-lined spectroscopic binary (SB2) provides both line-of-sight velocity amplitudes. Gaia adds precise one-dimensional astrometric information over a mission-length time baseline \citep{GaiaMission2016}.

The broad concept of combining Gaia or Hipparcos astrometry with relative astrometry and radial velocities is established. In particular, BINARYS jointly treats Hipparcos/Gaia absolute astrometry with relative astrometry and radial velocities, including resolved cases \citep{Leclerc2023}. The present problem is narrower. Gaia's published DR3 astrometric-binary processing explicitly applied preliminary filters to reduce contamination from partially resolved double stars \citep{Halbwachs2023}. Independently, \citet{Holl2023} demonstrated that source structure and close companions can introduce scan-angle-dependent image-parameter biases, and \citet{ElBadry2024} included marginally resolved binaries in a forward generative model of the Gaia DR3 astrometric-orbit catalogue selection function. These results motivate an observation model in which a luminous binary is not assumed to produce the same photocentric coordinate at all projected separations.

We therefore investigate a candidate methodological gap rather than make a priority claim: target-level joint inference that combines independent resolved relative astrometry, both SB2 velocity curves, and Gaia-like epoch measurements with a scan-angle- and resolution-aware measurement response, while propagating all information to component masses, parallax and the Gaia-band light fraction. The immediate goal is not yet a calibrated Gaia image model. Gaia's actual PSF/LSF calibration has strong colour, time, and instrument-state dependence \citep{Rowell2021}; replacing it by a fixed Gaussian would not be physically justified. Instead, we use a deliberately transparent surrogate to ask a falsifiable question: does there exist a single-peak marginal-resolution regime in which fitting a photocentre model to data generated by a resolution-aware response produces a measurable and systematic mismatch?

This paper documents the complete validation chain developed for that question: physical orbit conventions, the unresolved photocentre limit, the mathematical multi-peak boundary of the surrogate, mission-grounded scan schedules, a full-sky transition experiment, and a matched-transit-count control that separates the effects of observation number from scan geometry.
